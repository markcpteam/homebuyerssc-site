A construction project next to your house does not stop you from selling, but it does narrow the buyer pool and it changes how you have to prepare. The houses that sell in this situation are the ones where the seller documented the project from public records and priced it, rather than hoping buyers would not notice.

This guide is about a specific, documentable project near a specific property. It does not attempt to characterize any neighborhood or area.

Homebuyers SC buys houses across Charleston, Berkeley, and Dorchester counties, including properties next to active construction.

## Why does nearby construction make a sale harder?

Not because of the noise, primarily. Because of uncertainty.

A retail buyer is making the largest purchase of their life and is being asked to accept an unknown: how long the work lasts, what gets built, and what it does to access and value. Faced with an unknown, most buyers move to the next listing rather than price it.

Specific ways it shows up:

- **Showings suffer.** Weekday showings during active work are unpleasant, and weekends are when buyers look.
- **Access changes.** Lane closures, detours, and blocked driveways during road work affect whether people can even reach the house.
- **Appraisal questions.** An appraiser may note the condition and adjust, or comparable sales nearby may already reflect it.
- **Financing is usually fine**, which is the good news. Unlike a structural or insurance problem, construction next door rarely stops a loan by itself.
- **Timeline risk for the buyer.** A project scheduled for eighteen months can run longer, and buyers know it.

## How do I find out what is actually being built?

This is the highest-value thing you can do, and all of it is public and free.

| What to check | Where | What you learn |
|---|---|---|
| State road projects | South Carolina Department of Transportation project information | Scope, schedule, and status of highway and road work |
| Local road and utility work | The county or municipal public works or engineering department | Smaller projects SCDOT does not handle |
| What is approved next door | County or city planning and zoning department, and permit records | What was actually approved, and for what use |
| Rezoning and development applications | Planning commission and council agendas and minutes, which are public | What is proposed but not yet approved |
| The parcel itself | County assessor and Register of Deeds records | Ownership and recorded easements affecting nearby land |

Bring back three things: what the project is, the approved or estimated completion timeline, and the source. A buyer who is handed a documented answer prices it. A buyer who is told "I think they are widening the road" imagines the worst.

## Do I have to disclose it?

Disclose what you know. Do not speculate about what you do not.

SC Code 27-50-40 requires a residential property condition disclosure statement from an owner, and the statute gives the owner the option to indicate actual knowledge of a condition or to make no representation about it. SC Code 27-50-30 exempts certain transfers, including transfers by a fiduciary administering an estate and foreclosure transfers.

The practical line most sellers should hold: state the facts you have verified from public records, provide the source, and do not characterize what the project will do to value or to the area. Facts help you. Predictions create exposure.

*Whether and how a particular off-site condition must be disclosed is a question for a licensed South Carolina attorney. Ask your closing attorney before completing any disclosure form.*

## Should I wait for the project to finish?

Sometimes, and it depends on three things you can find out.

| Factor | Points toward waiting | Points toward selling now |
|---|---|---|
| Remaining duration | Months, with a firm end date | Years, or no reliable end date |
| What is being built | Something that plausibly improves access or amenity | Ongoing heavy or commercial use |
| Your carrying cost | Low, and you can absorb it | Mortgage, taxes, and insurance are the pressure |
| Your timeline | Flexible | A job, a divorce, an estate, a foreclosure date |
| The house itself | Sound and financeable | Needs work anyway, which compounds the problem |

The honest version: waiting only pays if the project genuinely ends soon and the house is otherwise ready to sell. Carrying a house for two more years to avoid a discount is usually the more expensive choice, and construction timelines slip.

## How does an as-is cash sale work here?

The buyer values the house, accounts for the conditions including the project, and offers. No showings on a schedule, no repeated visits during work hours, no financed buyer who walks at week six.

In South Carolina the closing must be supervised by a licensed attorney, which is settled law from *State v. Buyers Service Co.*, 292 S.C. 426, 357 S.E.2d 15 (1987). Ask any buyer which closing attorney they use before accepting an offer. See [how to choose a cash home buyer in Charleston](/how-to-choose-a-cash-home-buyer/).

## Frequently asked questions

**Will nearby construction stop a buyer's financing?**

Usually not on its own. Lenders care about the property's condition and insurability. Construction next door is a value and desirability question, not typically a collateral one.

**Can I sue or claim compensation for the disruption?**

That depends entirely on the project, the parties, and whether any of your property is being taken. It is a question for an attorney, not for a web page, and the answer varies enormously.

**Should I lower my asking price or wait for an offer?**

Price it with the information you gathered. A listing that sits while buyers guess at the project usually ends up selling for less than one priced correctly on day one.

**What if the construction has already damaged my property?**

Document it, photograph it, and talk to an attorney before you sell, because the claim and the sale interact.

**What if there is ongoing noise or traffic rather than a project with an end date?**

Different situation, different approach. See [selling a house with ongoing noise or traffic issues](/guides/selling-a-house-with-ongoing-noise-or-traffic-issues/).

**How fast can a cash sale close?**

Usually one to three weeks once the closing attorney confirms clear title. See [how fast can I sell my house in South Carolina](/guides/how-fast-can-i-sell-my-house-in-south-carolina-timeline-guide/).
