You do not have to empty a house before selling it in South Carolina. A cash buyer can purchase it as-is with the contents still inside, and handle the clean-out after closing. For families dealing with a heavily full house, that is usually the difference between a sale that happens and one that keeps getting postponed.

Homebuyers SC has bought houses with contents included across Charleston, Berkeley, and Dorchester counties since 2017, including estates where nothing had been removed and rooms were not accessible.

## Why will a traditional buyer not purchase a full house?

The buyer usually would. Their lender will not.

A mortgage lender needs an appraisal and an inspection. Neither can be completed when rooms cannot be entered, the electrical panel cannot be reached, and the condition of floors, walls, and plumbing cannot be observed. An appraiser who cannot see the house cannot value it, and a lender will not fund a loan against a house it cannot value.

Three practical consequences follow:

- **Showings are limited or unsafe.** Blocked exits, stacked weight, and floor loading are genuine hazards, not squeamishness.
- **Insurance becomes a problem.** Carriers are already cautious in the Lowcountry. A house that cannot be inspected is frequently declined, and an uninsurable house is an unfinanceable one. See [selling a house you cannot insure anymore](/guides/selling-a-house-you-cant-insure-anymore/).
- **Code enforcement may already be involved.** Accumulation that is visible from outside, or that draws pest complaints, tends to generate citations that accrue while the house sits. See [selling a house with code violations](/guides/selling-a-house-with-code-violations-how-to-get-out-without-fixing-everything/).

## What does cleaning it out actually cost?

Run this before deciding, because the clean-out is usually the largest single expense in the whole situation and it is paid out of pocket, upfront, before any sale money arrives.

| What a clean-out involves | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| Labor | Specialty clean-out crews, priced by volume and by conditions, not by hours |
| Disposal | Multiple roll-off dumpsters, with tipping fees, and separate handling for anything hazardous |
| Sorting | Somebody has to decide what is kept. On an estate, that is usually the family, in person |
| Repairs revealed underneath | Floors, subfloor, plumbing, and wiring are frequently worse than expected once visible |
| Pest and moisture remediation | Common in the Lowcountry, and usually not discovered until the house is empty |
| Time | Weeks at minimum, during which taxes, insurance, and utilities continue |
| What you get for it | A house that can now be listed, at a price that still reflects its condition |

The trap is that a clean-out does not turn the house into a retail-ready house. It turns it into a house that needs repairs, which then have to be paid for as well before a lender will finance it. Two large expenses, in sequence, before a sale.

## How does an as-is, with-contents sale work?

The buyer values the house on what it will be worth once cleared and repaired, subtracts that cost and their margin, and offers the remainder. The contents transfer with the property, and disposal becomes the buyer's problem and expense.

What you are not asked to do: remove anything, clean anything, repair anything, or make the house presentable. One walkthrough is typically all the access required.

What you should still do: take out what matters to you. Documents, photographs, jewelry, anything with personal or financial significance. Once the deed transfers, the contents go with the house. Give yourself a real deadline for that step rather than an open one.

In South Carolina the closing must be supervised by a licensed attorney, which is settled law from *State v. Buyers Service Co.*, 292 S.C. 426, 357 S.E.2d 15 (1987). Ask any buyer which closing attorney they use before you accept an offer. [How to choose a cash home buyer in Charleston](/how-to-choose-a-cash-home-buyer/) covers the rest of the questions worth asking.

## Do I have to disclose the condition?

Yes. South Carolina requires a residential property disclosure covering known material defects, and selling as-is does not remove that obligation. The statute is SC Code 27-50-40, and SC Code 27-50-30 lists transfers that are exempt from it, including a transfer "by a fiduciary in the course of the administration of a decedent's estate, guardianship, conservatorship, or trust" and transfers by foreclosure sale. An exemption from the statutory form is not permission to conceal a known problem, and whether one applies to your sale is a question for your closing attorney.

In this situation it rarely causes friction. A buyer purchasing specifically because the house is full is not surprised by a disclosure saying so. What does cause problems is a defect discovered later that was known and not disclosed, which is how a closing gets renegotiated or falls apart two weeks out.

*The statutes cited here are the starting point, not the whole answer. How they apply turns on your specific facts, so confirm with a licensed South Carolina attorney before acting.*

## What if the house was inherited?

This is the common version of the situation. A parent's house, adult children living elsewhere, and nobody with the time or the standing to empty it.

Two things to sort out before a sale can close:

- **Authority.** Until the probate court appoints a personal representative, no one can sign a deed for the estate. That happens in the probate court of the county where the person died.
- **Distance.** If the heirs live out of state, the clean-out is close to impossible to manage remotely, which is precisely why a with-contents sale exists. See [selling an inherited house in South Carolina when you live out of state](/guides/selling-an-inherited-property-out-of-state-without-the-hassle/) and [selling a property after inheriting a messy estate](/guides/selling-a-property-after-inheriting-a-messy-estate-how-to-simplify-the-chaos/).

A note on handling it: hoarding is a recognized condition, not a character failure or a housekeeping lapse. If a family member still lives in the house, the sale timeline needs to account for that person having somewhere to go and time to get there. A buyer who will not flex on the closing date to accommodate that is the wrong buyer.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I have to remove anything at all?**

No, though you should remove what you want to keep. Everything left behind transfers with the house.

**Will the offer be lower because of the contents?**

Yes. The offer reflects the clean-out cost and the repairs underneath. The comparison worth making is not against a renovated house, it is against what you would net after paying for the clean-out and the repairs yourself, months later.

**What if there are pests, mold, or biohazard conditions?**

Those are priced in rather than disqualifying. Say so up front, because it affects the offer and a buyer who finds out late will renegotiate.

**What if utilities are shut off?**

Not an obstacle for a cash sale. It matters for a walkthrough, so mention it in advance.

**How fast can it close?**

Usually one to three weeks once the closing attorney confirms clear title. Probate, liens, and occupancy questions extend that.

**What if someone is still living there?**

The sale can still happen, and the closing date can usually be set around their move. Raise it early rather than at the contract stage.
