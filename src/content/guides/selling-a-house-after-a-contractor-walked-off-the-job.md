A half-finished renovation is worse than an untouched house, because open walls, disconnected systems, and unfinished work make the property unfinanceable. The buyer pool collapses to people who pay cash.

You can sell it in that state. Before you do, there are two South Carolina things to check, and one of them has a ninety-day clock on it.

Homebuyers SC buys houses mid-renovation across Charleston, Berkeley, and Dorchester counties.

## First: is there a lien, or could there be?

South Carolina puts a hard deadline on mechanics liens, and it works in your favor more often than people expect.

Under SC Code 29-5-90, a mechanics lien "shall be dissolved unless" the claimant serves and files the required statement "within ninety days after he ceases to labor on or furnish labor or materials." Miss that window and the lien dissolves.

Two consequences:

- If a contractor threatened a lien months ago and never filed, there may be nothing recorded.
- If one was filed, it is on the record, the closing attorney's search finds it, and it is paid or negotiated before closing.

Unpaid subcontractors and suppliers are the ones to think about, not just your general contractor. They can have their own claims even if you paid the contractor in full.

*Lien rights and deadlines are fact-specific, and the ninety-day figure is the start of the analysis rather than the whole of it. Talk to a licensed South Carolina attorney about your situation before relying on it.*

## Second: was the contractor licensed?

South Carolina requires a residential builder license for work on a residential building "when the cost of the undertaking exceeds five thousand dollars," regulated by the South Carolina Residential Builders Commission under the Department of Labor, Licensing and Regulation.

Two things you can do, both free:

- **Look the contractor up** in LLR's licensee lookup to see whether the license is real and active, and check Board Orders for disciplinary history.
- **File a complaint** with the Residential Builders Commission if the work or the conduct warrants it.

That is separate from any money claim, which is a civil matter and an attorney question. It is worth doing regardless, and it is worth doing before you sell, because afterward you have less reason to and the next homeowner has no standing.

## Why will a lender not finance a half-finished house?

Because the collateral is incomplete and cannot be valued or insured as a finished house.

| What stops it | Why |
|---|---|
| Missing or disconnected systems | Many loan programs require functioning heat, electrical, and plumbing |
| Open walls and unfinished structure | An appraiser cannot value it, an inspector cannot complete a report |
| Unfinished permitted work | An open permit shows in the record and generally has to be resolved |
| Insurance | Carriers are cautious about a property under construction and vacant |
| Unknown scope | Nobody knows what is behind the unfinished work until it is opened further |

The last row is what actually sets the price. A buyer is pricing the completion plus the discovery risk, and the more documentation you can hand over, the less they have to guess.

## What should I gather before getting offers?

This is the highest-return hour you can spend.

- **The contract and any change orders**
- **Proof of what you paid**, and to whom
- **Permits pulled**, and their status. An open permit is a fact the buyer needs
- **Plans and specifications**, if they exist
- **Inspection records** from any work already approved
- **Photographs** of what is behind the walls before anything was closed up
- **Contact details for subcontractors**, and whether any of them were left unpaid
- **Any lien notices** you have received

Handing a buyer a documented half-finished project gets a materially better price than handing them a mystery, because the discount for the unknown is always larger than the discount for a known problem.

## Should I finish the work first?

Sometimes, and be honest about what finishing involves now.

| Consideration | Reality |
|---|---|
| A new contractor | Many will not take over another builder's unfinished work, and those who will price the risk |
| Scope | The second contractor will want to open up what the first one did |
| Permits | Existing permits may need transfer, revision, or reinspection |
| Cost | Higher than the original quote, routinely |
| Time | Longer than the original schedule, routinely |
| Financing the finish | Hard, because the house securing any loan is the unfinished house |

Finishing makes sense when the remaining work is bounded, you have the cash without borrowing against the house, and completion restores financeability on a property worth materially more finished. Otherwise you are funding a project to sell a house you have already decided to leave.

## How does an as-is sale work mid-project?

The buyer prices the completion, the unknowns, and their margin, and takes the project on. You are not asked to finish anything, close any walls, or resolve the contractor dispute first.

Any recorded lien is paid from the proceeds at closing by the closing attorney, who in South Carolina must supervise the closing under *State v. Buyers Service Co.*, 292 S.C. 426, 357 S.E.2d 15 (1987). Whether you keep any claim against the contractor is a contract term, so raise it rather than assume it. ## Frequently asked questions

**Can I sell while I am in a dispute with the contractor?**

Usually yes. A recorded lien is dealt with at closing. Pending litigation is more complicated, particularly if a lis pendens has been filed. See [selling a house with ongoing legal disputes](/guides/selling-a-house-with-ongoing-legal-disputes/).

**Do I keep the right to sue the contractor after selling?**

It depends what the purchase contract says. Decide it deliberately and get it in writing rather than assuming.

**What if subcontractors were never paid?**

They may have their own claims even if you paid the contractor. Raise it with the closing attorney early so the title search is not the first anyone hears of it.

**What about the open permit?**

It shows in the building department record and generally has to be resolved at some point. Tell the buyer. See [selling a house with unpermitted work](/guides/selling-a-house-with-unpermitted-work-what-homeowners-should-know/).

**Will I lose everything I put in?**

You will not recover it all. The comparison worth making is against what finishing would cost from here plus more months of carrying the house, not against what you have already spent, which is gone either way.

**How fast can it close?**

Usually one to three weeks once the closing attorney confirms clear title. Liens are what most often add time. See [how fast can I sell my house in South Carolina](/guides/how-fast-can-i-sell-my-house-in-south-carolina-timeline-guide/).
