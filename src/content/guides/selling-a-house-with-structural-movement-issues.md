Structural movement is common in the Lowcountry and it does not prevent a sale. What it prevents is a sale to a buyer using a mortgage, because a lender will not accept compromised collateral.

The question a buyer and an engineer will both ask first is whether the movement is **active or historic**, and it is worth understanding the difference before you talk to anyone.

Homebuyers SC has bought houses with settlement, failed piers, and ongoing movement across Charleston, Berkeley, and Dorchester counties since 2017.

## Why is movement so common here?

It is about what the house sits on rather than how it was built.

- **Soil.** Much of the tri-county area sits on soft, saturated, or filled ground, including marsh fill in parts of the peninsula and along the tidal creeks. Soft soil compresses unevenly, and uneven compression is what movement looks like from inside.
- **Water table.** High groundwater and heavy seasonal rain move moisture through the soil repeatedly. Soil that swells and shrinks moves what sits on it.
- **Crawlspace construction.** Much older Lowcountry housing is pier-and-beam over a crawlspace. Piers settle, masonry piers deteriorate in persistent moisture, and sill and floor framing above them rots.
- **Age.** Houses built before modern footing requirements were not engineered for the ground they occupy.

## Active or historic: what is the difference?

| | Historic movement | Active movement |
|---|---|---|
| What it means | The house moved, then stabilized | It is still moving |
| Typical signs | Old cracks that have not changed, doors adjusted once and fine since | Cracks reopening after repair, doors that stop latching again, floors getting worse |
| How it is established | An engineer's assessment, and monitoring over time | The same, plus evidence of change |
| Effect on repair | Repair addresses the damage | Repair has to address the cause, or it recurs |
| Effect on the sale | Priced, and disclosable | Priced more heavily, and disclosable |

Both are sellable. Neither is a reason a cash buyer walks away. But an owner who says "it settled years ago and stopped" and cannot show anything is asking a buyer to take the more expensive assumption.

## What does it do to a retail sale?

The lender decides, not the buyer.

When an appraiser or inspector flags movement, the lender treats its security as compromised and either declines the loan or requires the repair before closing. The buyer may want the house and be unable to buy it, which is why price reductions do not rescue these deals.

Insurance compounds it. Standard homeowners policies exclude earth movement, and a carrier seeing active movement on an older or flood-exposed house may decline or non-renew. An uninsurable house is an unfinanceable one independently. See [selling a house you cannot insure anymore](/guides/selling-a-house-you-cant-insure-anymore/).

## Should I repair it first?

Run the arithmetic, because the scope grows reliably in this category.

| Step | What it involves |
|---|---|
| Structural engineer | Paid upfront, and most reputable contractors will not scope without one |
| Cause versus symptom | With active movement, drainage and moisture usually have to be addressed too |
| Contractor bids | Specialized work, and bids for the same house vary widely in method and price |
| Permits | Structural work is permitted work across the tri-county area |
| What is found underneath | Crawlspace work routinely uncovers rot, moisture, plumbing, or pest damage |
| Time | Weeks to months, carrying the house throughout |
| Disclosure afterward | Repaired movement is still disclosable and still discounted |
| Net effect | Repair restores financeability. It rarely adds value beyond its cost |

Repairing makes sense with cash on hand, time to wait, and a house otherwise strong enough that restoring financeability unlocks a materially higher price. For most owners facing this, one of those three is missing. See [do I need repairs before selling as-is](/guides/do-i-need-repairs-before-selling-as-is-an-sc-sellers-guide/).

## How does an as-is sale work?

The buyer estimates what the house is worth once stable and repaired, subtracts that cost and their margin, and offers the remainder. No lender, so no lender condition. You are not asked to get an engineer's report, pull permits, or repair anything.

Disclose what you know. SC Code 27-50-40 requires a residential property condition disclosure statement covering categories including structural components, and lets an owner indicate actual knowledge or make no representation. SC Code 27-50-30 exempts certain transfers, including transfers by a fiduciary administering a decedent's estate and foreclosure sales.

*Whether and how disclosure applies to your sale is a question for a licensed South Carolina attorney. Ask your closing attorney before completing the form.*

In South Carolina the closing must be supervised by a licensed attorney, from *State v. Buyers Service Co.*, 292 S.C. 426, 357 S.E.2d 15 (1987). See [how to choose a cash home buyer in Charleston](/how-to-choose-a-cash-home-buyer/).

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need an engineer's report to sell?**

Not for a cash sale. A cash buyer does their own assessment. An engineer's report is what a lender requires, which is a path you are stepping off.

**Will an investor still want it?**

This is a category cash buyers specifically look for, because the discount is predictable and the repair is a known trade.

**How much less will I get?**

The offer reflects the repair cost plus margin. Compare it against the engineer, the repair, the overruns, and more months of carrying costs, not against a renovated house.

**What if the movement is in the crawlspace rather than visible inside?**

Common here, and it is the same conversation. Mention any standing water or moisture, because it is found immediately anyway. See [selling a house with foundation problems](/guides/selling-a-house-with-foundation-problems/).

**What if there is framing or floor damage too?**

They travel together in crawlspace houses. See [selling a house with structural issues in Charleston](/guides/selling-a-property-with-structural-issues-how-to-sell-when-buyers-say-no/).

**How fast can it close?**

Usually one to three weeks once the closing attorney confirms clear title. See [how fast can I sell my house in South Carolina](/guides/how-fast-can-i-sell-my-house-in-south-carolina-timeline-guide/).
