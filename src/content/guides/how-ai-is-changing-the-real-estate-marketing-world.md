An automated home value estimate is a statistical guess built from public records and nearby sales. It has never been inside your house, and that is the whole problem.

For a well-maintained house in a subdivision of similar houses, the guess is often reasonable. For a house with a failing roof, a wet crawlspace, an unpermitted addition, or forty years of deferred maintenance, it is frequently wrong by a wide margin, and wrong in a direction that costs sellers time.

Homebuyers SC values houses by looking at them, across Charleston, Berkeley, and Dorchester counties.

## What is an automated estimate actually built from?

| Input | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| Recorded sale prices nearby | County Register of Deeds, public record |
| Square footage, bed and bath counts | The county assessor's record |
| Lot size and year built | Public record |
| Recent listing data | Where available |
| Tax assessment | Public record |

Every one of those is a record about the house. None of them is an observation of it.

## What can it not see?

This is the list that matters if your house needs work.

- **The roof's actual condition**, which is the most common insurance trigger in this market
- **The crawlspace.** Moisture, failed piers, rot, and standing water are invisible from the street and common in older Lowcountry housing
- **Whether the systems work.** Heat, electrical, plumbing
- **Water intrusion and mold**
- **Structural movement**
- **Whether the house is insurable at all**, which decides whether anyone can finance it
- **Unpermitted square footage.** If the assessor's record shows less than what exists, the estimate is built on the smaller number. If the record shows more than is permitted, the estimate overstates what an appraiser will count
- **The contents**, if the house is full
- **Occupancy**, if there is a tenant or an unauthorized occupant

## Why does the assessor's square footage matter so much?

Because it is an input to the estimate and to an appraisal, and it is frequently wrong on houses that have been altered.

An enclosed porch, a converted garage, or an addition built without a permit may not appear in the county record. That creates two problems at once: an automated estimate that does not count the space, and an appraisal that will not count it either when a buyer tries to finance the purchase.

See [selling a house with unpermitted work](/guides/selling-a-house-with-unpermitted-work-what-homeowners-should-know/). Pulling your own permit record from the building department, and comparing it against the assessor's square footage, is free and takes one phone call.

## How is a house that needs work actually valued?

By arithmetic rather than by comparison, and you can run it yourself.

1. **What the house would be worth repaired**, based on comparable renovated sales nearby. This is where an automated estimate is genuinely useful, because it is describing finished houses.
2. **Minus the repair cost.** The full scope, including what gets discovered once walls open.
3. **Minus carrying costs during the work.** Taxes, insurance, utilities, and financing for the period.
4. **Minus a contingency**, because renovation scopes grow.
5. **Minus the buyer's margin.** This is a business.

What is left is what a cash buyer can pay. A buyer who will not walk you through those five lines is asking you to trust a number you cannot check. See [how to choose a cash home buyer in Charleston](/how-to-choose-a-cash-home-buyer/).

Notice that step one is the online estimate's number, and steps two through five are everything it cannot see. That gap is not the buyer being unfair. It is the cost of the work.

## Should I ignore online estimates entirely?

No. Use them for what they are good at.

| Good for | Not good for |
|---|---|
| A rough sense of the finished value of similar houses | Pricing a house that needs work |
| Seeing what nearby houses actually sold for | Anything about condition |
| Noticing that the assessor's square footage looks wrong | Insurability, which decides financeability |
| A starting point for a conversation | A number to reject an offer against |

The providers publish their own accuracy figures, usually as a median error rate, and those are worth looking up on their sites. Note that a median means half of estimates are off by more than that, and houses in poor condition are exactly the ones in that half.

## What should I do instead?

- **Get the permit record** from the building department and compare it with the assessor's square footage
- **Get an insurance quote.** If you cannot get one, that is the single most important fact about your house and no estimate will tell you
- **Get a written repair estimate** for anything major, so you know whether the scope is bounded. See [do I need repairs before selling as-is](/guides/do-i-need-repairs-before-selling-as-is-an-sc-sellers-guide/)
- **Ask an agent for comparable sales**, both renovated and as-is, in your specific area
- **Get more than one written offer**, and ask each buyer to show their arithmetic

## Frequently asked questions

**Why is the estimate so much higher than the offers I am getting?**

Almost always because the estimate is describing a house in normal condition and the offers are pricing yours as it is. The gap is the repair cost plus the buyer's margin.

**Why is the estimate lower than I expected?**

Frequently the assessor's square footage is wrong, often because of unpermitted space. Pull the record and check.

**Does the tax assessment tell me what my house is worth?**

No. An assessment is for taxation and is not a market valuation. Under SC Code 12-43-220 the assessment ratio itself differs by use, 4% for an owner-occupied legal residence and 6% for other real property, which is a separate matter again.

**Should I get an appraisal before selling?**

For a cash sale, no. An appraisal is a lender's requirement. For a disputed value among heirs or in a divorce, a neutral appraisal is frequently worth it.

**Can I trust an estimate on a house that has never been renovated?**

Treat it as the finished-value input in step one above, not as what your house would fetch today.

**How fast can a cash sale close?**

Usually one to three weeks once the closing attorney confirms clear title. In South Carolina that supervision is required under *State v. Buyers Service Co.*, 292 S.C. 426, 357 S.E.2d 15 (1987). See [how fast can I sell my house in South Carolina](/guides/how-fast-can-i-sell-my-house-in-south-carolina-timeline-guide/).
