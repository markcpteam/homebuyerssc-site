A house that needs to come back to studs is not a repair problem. It is a construction project, and it sells to people who do construction projects.

That narrows the buyer pool to cash, and it changes what the house is valued on: land, plus whatever the remaining structure is worth, minus the cost of the work and the buyer's margin. Understanding that formula is what lets you judge whether an offer is reasonable.

Homebuyers SC buys gut rehab properties across Charleston, Berkeley, and Dorchester counties.

## What makes a house a gut rehab rather than a fixer?

Usually several of these at once:

- Systems at or past end of life: wiring, plumbing, HVAC
- A roof that has been failing long enough to damage what is under it
- Sustained water intrusion, and the mold and rot that follow
- Structural movement, failed piers, or compromised framing
- Long vacancy in a climate that punishes it
- Fire, storm, or flood damage
- Work started and abandoned partway

One of these is a repair. Four is a rehab, because they interact: you cannot replace wiring without opening walls, and once walls are open the rest is exposed.

## Why will no lender finance it?

Every route is blocked at once.

| Obstacle | Why |
|---|---|
| No functioning systems | Many loan programs require working heat, electrical, and plumbing |
| Uninsurable | Carriers decline, and no insurance means no mortgage |
| Appraisal | Difficult to value, and appraisers commonly require completed repairs |
| Habitability | A house nobody can live in fails condition requirements |
| Unknown scope | The full extent is not known until demolition |

Renovation loan programs exist and carry their own requirements, contractor approvals, and timelines, and the property still has to qualify. A buyer using one is not a fast or certain buyer. See [selling a house no lender will finance](/guides/selling-a-house-no-one-will-finance-your-best-options/).

## How does a buyer actually price it?

Work it through yourself so you can judge an offer.

1. **What the finished house would be worth**, based on comparable renovated sales nearby, not on a national number.
2. **Minus the rehab cost.** The full scope, including what gets discovered.
3. **Minus carrying costs during the work.** Taxes, insurance, utilities, and financing for months.
4. **Minus a contingency.** Gut rehabs run over. Every experienced buyer holds some back.
5. **Minus their margin.** This is a business.

What is left is the offer. A buyer who will not walk you through those five lines is asking you to trust a number you cannot check. See [how to choose a cash home buyer in Charleston](/how-to-choose-a-cash-home-buyer/).

## What moves the number up?

Reducing what the buyer has to guess about.

- **Access.** Let them see everything, including the crawlspace and attic. Concealment reads as a worse problem than whatever is there.
- **Any engineering or inspection report** you already have.
- **The permit record.** Pull it from the building department. Unpermitted additions and open permits affect what the buyer can do.
- **Utilities on**, if feasible, so systems can be assessed rather than assumed.
- **Documented history.** When the roof failed, when the water started, what was already attempted.
- **Title clarity.** A long-held or inherited property is where undocumented ownership shows up. See [selling a house with title problems](/guides/selling-a-house-with-title-problems-how-to-close-when-ownership-isnt-clear/).

What moves it down is a locked room, an unknown, or a surprise after the contract.

## Is it worth doing some of the work first?

Almost never, in this category.

Partial work on a gut rehab tends to be redone by whoever finishes it, so the money does not carry into the price. A new kitchen in a house that needs rewiring is a kitchen that comes out when the walls open. The exception is work that stops active damage, such as tarping a roof or stopping water, because that prevents the number from getting worse while you sell.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is my house worth anything at all?**

Almost certainly, because the land has value and in much of the Charleston area that is a significant share of the total. What you are selling is the lot plus a structure with some remaining value, minus the work.

**Should I demolish it myself?**

Rarely. Demolition costs money, may need a permit, and removes whatever value the structure had. Let a buyer decide what stays.

**What if it is in a historic district?**

Then what can be demolished or altered is controlled, and that affects value in both directions. Check with the local review board, because it changes what any buyer can do.

**What if the house is full as well?**

Contents transfer with a cash sale. See [selling a hoarder house without cleaning it out](/guides/selling-a-hoarder-house-how-to-sell-without-cleaning-everything-out/).

**Do I have to disclose everything?**

Disclose what you know. SC Code 27-50-40 requires a residential property condition disclosure statement, and SC Code 27-50-30 exempts certain transfers. Ask your closing attorney what applies. On a gut rehab a buyer expects problems, so disclosure rarely costs you anything and concealment costs a lot.

**How fast can it close?**

Usually one to three weeks once the closing attorney confirms clear title. In South Carolina that supervision is required under *State v. Buyers Service Co.*, 292 S.C. 426, 357 S.E.2d 15 (1987).
